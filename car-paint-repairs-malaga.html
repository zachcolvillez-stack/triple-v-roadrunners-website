Colour matching, and why it is the hard part

Getting a repair to disappear is mostly about the paint, not the panel. Factory colours are not one fixed recipe — the same colour code can vary between production batches and between plants, and every car on the road has faded from UV exposure since it left the factory. A Perth car that has spent years parked outside has moved a long way from its original code.

So matching means working from the vehicle in front of us, not just the code on the compliance plate. That involves reading the existing paint, mixing to suit it, and spraying out a test card to check the match before anything goes near the car.

The other half is blending. On a metallic or pearl finish especially, paint is carried out into the adjacent panel and faded so there is no hard edge where new meets old. A repair that is technically the right colour but stops dead at a panel line will still be visible. That blending work is a large part of why a quality refinish takes the time it does.

Perth sun, and why paint fails here

UV exposure is the main thing working against automotive paint in Perth. Sustained sun breaks down the clear coat over years, and once the clear starts to go the colour underneath is no longer protected. The usual progression is a dulling of gloss, then a chalky look, then clear coat lifting and peeling in patches — most often on the roof, bonnet and boot lid, because they take the most direct exposure.

Two practical consequences. First, damage that has broken through the clear coat is worth attending to rather than leaving, because it is now a path for further degradation and, on steel, corrosion. Second, a car that has faded is exactly why colour matching has to be done off the vehicle rather than off the paint code.

Preparation and quality control

Paint only ever looks as good as what is underneath it. High-gloss automotive finishes are unforgiving — any imperfection in the surface below shows through, and often shows up worse once the clear coat goes on and starts reflecting light.

  • Surface preparation — stripping back the damaged area, filling and shaping where required, then progressively finer sanding to a flat, even surface.
  • Masking and protection so overspray does not reach glass, trim, seals or adjacent panels.
  • Priming the prepared area so the topcoat has a consistent base to sit on.
  • Colour and clear coat, blended into the adjacent panels where the finish requires it.
  • Curing — paint needs time to harden properly, and rushing it is how a finish gets marked before the customer has even driven away.
  • Final inspection — panel gaps and alignment checked, the finish checked in good light for texture and match, and the vehicle cleaned before handover.

How the inspection and quote works

Every job starts the same way: we look at the vehicle in person. Photos are useful for a rough idea over the phone, but panel damage hides things — a dent that looks superficial can have a cracked bracket or a distorted mounting point behind it, and a scuff can turn out to have gone through to bare metal.

  • Bring the vehicle in, or send photos first if you want an initial idea before making the trip.
  • We inspect the damage and check what is behind the visible panel — mounts, brackets, clips, adjacent panels and panel gaps.
  • We work out repair versus replace for each damaged part, and explain why.
  • You get a written quote itemising the panel work, parts, preparation and refinishing.
  • Nothing extra happens without your say-so. If hidden damage turns up once the panel is off, you get a phone call and a revised quote to approve — not a surprise on the final invoice.

On hidden damage: it is common, and any repairer who tells you a collision quote is guaranteed final before dismantling is guessing. We would rather explain that up front than have the conversation later.

Frequently asked questions

Can you match my vehicle's existing paint colour?

Yes. We match against the paint on your vehicle as it is now, not just the code on the compliance plate, because paint fades with UV exposure and factory colours vary between batches. We spray out a test card and check the match before any paint goes on the car.

Can scratches be repaired without repainting the whole car?

Usually, yes. A scratch that has only marked the clear coat can sometimes be polished out. Deeper scratches need the area prepared and refinished, with the paint blended into the surrounding panel — not the whole vehicle repainted.

Why do repaired panels sometimes not match?

The usual causes are matching to the paint code instead of the faded paint on the car, not blending into adjacent panels on a metallic or pearl finish, or inadequate surface preparation showing through the topcoat. All three are avoidable, and all three are why a cheaper quote is sometimes cheaper.

How long does a paint repair take?

Preparation, spraying and curing all take time, and curing in particular cannot be rushed without risking the finish. A localised repair is a much shorter job than a multi-panel refinish. We confirm the timeframe when we quote.

My clear coat is peeling — can that be fixed?

Yes. Peeling clear coat means the affected area needs to be prepared back and refinished; polishing will not recover it. It is common on Perth cars, usually on the roof, bonnet and boot, and it is worth addressing before the colour underneath is left unprotected.
